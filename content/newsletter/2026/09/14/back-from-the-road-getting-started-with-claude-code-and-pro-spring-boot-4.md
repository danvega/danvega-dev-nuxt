Happy Monday, friends!

It's been a busy couple of weeks on the road. Between Commit Your Code in Texas and KCDC in Kansas City, I've had the chance to give some talks, record a few podcasts, and spend time with so many great people in the developer community.

I'm home for a couple of weeks now, catching up on work and everything else that piles up while you're traveling. ## Conference Recap

### Commit Your Code

The first stop was [Commit Your Code](https://www.commityourcode.com/) in Plano, Texas, just outside Dallas. Danny Thompson and the team have put together something really special here.

A ticket was just $40, and the value you get for that is incredible. Great speakers, great food, and a welcoming community. One thing that stood out to me was the dedicated networking tracks. Having time built into the schedule specifically to meet and talk with other people was a really nice touch.

We recently had Danny on Spring Office Hours, where we talked about his work and the conference. If you missed that conversation, you can catch [S5E20: The Developer's Guide to AI with Danny Thompson](https://springofficehours.io/episodes/s5e20-the-developers-guide-to-ai-with-danny-thompson).

I got to see a bunch of great sessions. One of my favorites was the second-day keynote on how the PGA Tour uses AI. I really enjoyed hearing how they approached bringing AI into their workflows and applications. Seeing how a team is putting these tools to work gives you a lot to think about for your own projects.

I also gave a talk introducing Spring AI. The challenge? I had 25 minutes. If you've seen me talk about Spring AI before, you know I can spend a lot longer than that on the subject.

That time limit forced me to focus on the questions that matter when you're getting started: why AI matters for Java developers, why you would reach for a framework like Spring AI, and what it helps you do. I was happy with how it came together, and I got some really good feedback afterward.

Overall, a wonderful conference. Thank you to Danny, the organizers, and everyone who made it such a great experience.

### KCDC

Next up was [KCDC](https://www.kcdc.info/), and I just love this conference. I've been going for the last few years, and it's always an event I look forward to.

I was there for about four days, which gave me some time to check out workshops, record podcasts, and catch up with friends before and between my own sessions.

The big one for me this year was a brand-new talk: **Claude Code: Start Simple, Scale Smart**.

I put a lot of work into this presentation, and I honestly wasn't sure what to expect. At a conference with developers from so many different backgrounds, I wanted to cover something that would be useful across languages and frameworks.

We have all these new tools available to us, but how do we actually get started? How do we use them well? And how do we keep from getting overwhelmed when we open social media and see someone talking about running ten agents or reviewing pull requests from the gym?

My goal was to give people an approachable place to begin and a path to build on as they got more comfortable. Still, I wondered whether it would be too introductory for the audience.

The room ended up being packed, with people standing in the back. And the feedback afterward meant a lot to me. People told me it was exactly the introduction they had been looking for and that they now had a better idea of how to get started and when to take the next step.

After spending so much time putting this talk together, hearing that was incredibly rewarding. Thank you to everyone who came, asked questions, and shared feedback with me.

On Friday, I also gave **What's New in Spring**, walking through what's new in Spring Boot and what those changes mean for developers. We spent some time talking about why staying current matters, too, especially as AI becomes part of the conversation around finding vulnerabilities in software.

Two very different talks, and I really enjoyed getting to share both of them.

## Portland Java User Group

A few weeks ago, I also had the opportunity to speak virtually to the Portland Java User Group. I couldn't make it out to Portland this time, but I hope to visit in person someday soon.

The talk was an introduction to AI for Java developers using Spring AI. A theme that keeps coming up in these sessions is the idea that AI development means Python. That association makes sense when you think about the history of machine learning and deep learning, but many of us are now building applications that consume AI models.

If you're a Java developer, you can bring those capabilities into the applications you're already building. A framework like Spring AI helps with a lot of the integration work.

We started with some high-level concepts and then jumped into code to explore a few Spring AI features. My goal was to make it approachable and show how easy it can be to get something up and running.

Thank you again to the Portland Java User Group for having me. If you run a user group and would like me to present, virtually or in person, please reach out. I'd love to hear from you.
